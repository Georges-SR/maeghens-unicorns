A field guide to the animal that never was

Maeghen's Unicorns

Four thousand years of seals, scrolls, tapestries, thrones and stories — about a creature no one has ever seen, and no one has ever forgotten.

O dieses ist das Tier, das es nicht gibt.

“Oh, this is the creature that does not exist.”

Rainer Maria Rilke, Sonnets to Orpheus II.4, 1922

And yet it exists everywhere: stamped into clay in the Indus Valley, described by Greek physicians, hunted across medieval tapestries, chained to the royal arms of Scotland, and galloping through novels, films and toy boxes. This is its story — where it came from, what it meant, and why it endures.

I · Origins

Four thousand years
of a single horn

2600 BCE

  1. c. 2600–1900 BCE

    The seals of the Indus

    In Mohenjo-daro and Harappa, a one-horned, bull-like beast is the most common image on the thousands of stamp seals found — usually shown before a mysterious ritual stand. What it meant, no one can read: the script beside it is still undeciphered.

  2. c. 400 BCE

    Ctesias’ wild asses of India

    The Greek physician Ctesias of Cnidus, who never visited India, records “wild asses” as large as horses with white bodies, dark-red heads and blue eyes. Their single horn is white at the base, black in the middle and crimson at the tip — and drinking from it protects against poison.

  3. 4th century BCE

    Aristotle takes note

    In his studies of animals, Aristotle names two one-horned creatures — the oryx and the “Indian ass” — lending the unicorn the most respectable citation in the ancient world.

  4. 77 CE

    Pliny’s monoceros

    Pliny the Elder’s Natural History describes the fiercest animal of all: a horse’s body, a stag’s head, an elephant’s feet, a boar’s tail, and one black horn two cubits long. It “cannot be taken alive.”

  5. 2nd–4th century CE

    The Physiologus

    An anonymous Greek text from Alexandria gives the legend its most famous twist: no hunter can catch the unicorn — but it will lay its head in the lap of a virgin. Christian readers made the tale an allegory of Christ, and the image spread across Europe.

  6. c. 1200

    The bestiaries

    Illuminated “books of beasts”, like the Aberdeen Bestiary, place the unicorn beside the lion and the elephant as a real animal — drawn small, goat-like and gilded, a creature of moral lessons rather than zoology.

  7. 1414

    A giraffe for the emperor

    Envoys from Bengal bring a giraffe to the Ming court in Nanjing. Courtiers hail it as a qilin — the auspicious one-horned beast said to appear only under a wise ruler — and the court painter Shen Du records the event.

  8. c. 1495–1505

    The great tapestries

    Weavers of the Southern Netherlands produce the two most famous unicorn images ever made: the seven Unicorn Tapestries now at The Met Cloisters in New York, and the six Lady and the Unicorn hangings now in Paris’s Musée de Cluny.

  9. c. 1505–06

    Raphael’s hidden unicorn

    Raphael paints a young woman holding a tiny unicorn, a symbol of chastity. Later painters covered it over to turn her into Saint Catherine; the unicorn was only rediscovered during a restoration in the 1930s.

  10. 1603

    The lion and the unicorn

    Scotland’s royal arms had been held by unicorns since the 15th century. When James VI of Scotland inherits England’s throne, the Scottish unicorn joins the English lion on the royal coat of arms — where, chained, it still stands.

  11. 1612

    Into the stars

    The Dutch cartographer Petrus Plancius charts Monoceros, a faint constellation between Orion and Hydra. The unicorn has been in the night sky ever since — look up at the top of this page.

  12. 1638

    Ole Worm and the narwhal

    For centuries, “unicorn horns” — alicorns — sold for more than their weight in gold as antidotes to poison. The Danish naturalist Ole Worm demonstrates that they are in fact the tusks of the Arctic narwhal.

  13. 1662–1671

    A throne of “horn”

    Unconvinced, or simply dazzled, Denmark’s kings commission a coronation throne built from narwhal tusks — the Throne Chair of Denmark, still guarded by three silver lions at Rosenborg Castle.

  14. Rediscovered 2018

    The Siberian “unicorn”

    Fossils show that Elasmotherium sibiricum — a woolly, multi-tonne rhinoceros with a single great horn — survived on the Eurasian steppe until at least 39,000 years ago, sharing the world with early modern humans.

II · Across cultures

One horn, many kingdoms

Almost every great civilization imagined a one-horned animal. They did not agree on what it was — only that it was rare, strong, and meant something.

א

Hebrew Bible

The Re’em

A powerful wild beast of the Hebrew scriptures. Greek translators rendered it monokeros, “one-horn”, and so the King James Bible of 1611 is full of unicorns. Scholars now think the re’em was the aurochs, the great wild ox.

ك

Persia & the Arab world

The Karkadann

No gentle maiden-tamer: the karkadann of Persian and Arabic writers is a ferocious, rhinoceros-like monster that can skewer an elephant. In some tellings only the ring-dove can calm it, perching unharmed on its horn.

麒

China

The Qilin

A scaled, gentle chimera whose appearance announces a sage ruler or a great birth. Legend says a qilin visited Confucius’ mother before he was born — and that the slaying of one, in 481 BCE, made the old master lay down his brush.

獬

China · Korea · Japan

The Xiezhi

A one-horned beast of justice that can tell the honest from the guilty — and butts the guilty with its horn. As the haetae of Korea and the kaichi of Japan, it still guards courts and palaces.

✦

Medieval Europe

The Alicorn

Unicorn horn was the ultimate antidote, sold as powder and carved into cups that would sweat at the touch of poison. Queen Elizabeth I’s horn was reputedly valued at £10,000 — the price of a castle. Nearly all were narwhal tusks.

⚜

Scotland

The national animal

Proud, untameable and pure, the unicorn is Scotland’s national animal. On its coats of arms it is shown chained — a creature so wild that only a Scottish king could hold it.

III · Close reading

Read the tapestry

The Unicorn in Captivity, woven around 1495–1505, is perhaps the most famous unicorn image in the world. Every detail is a sign. Select a point of light to decode it.

The Met Cloisters, New York

A captive by choice?

The unicorn sits inside a fence it could easily leap. Is this the risen Christ, or a lover, happily tamed? Medieval viewers were comfortable holding both meanings at once. Choose a glowing point to begin.

Detail · 1 of 8

The horn

Long, straight and spiralled — clearly modelled on the narwhal tusks that circulated in Europe as “alicorns”. Weavers knew exactly what a unicorn horn looked like, because they could see one in a cathedral treasury.

Detail · 2 of 8

The pomegranate tree

Bursting with seeds, the pomegranate was a symbol of fertility and marriage — and, in Christian art, of the Resurrection and the unity of the Church. The unicorn is tethered to it.

Detail · 3 of 8

The monogram

The letters A and a reversed E, tied together with a cord, appear in the tree and in the corners. They surely name the couple who commissioned the set — but who they were is still a mystery.

Detail · 4 of 8

Collar & chain

A jewelled collar binds the unicorn to the tree by a slender gold chain. The tether is decorative rather than cruel: a sign of devotion, the bond of a faithful lover.

Detail · 5 of 8

The red stains

Are they wounds from the hunt in the other tapestries? Most scholars read them instead as juice dripping from the ripe pomegranates above — not blood, but a sign of fruitfulness.

Detail · 6 of 8

Beard, hooves & tail

Look closely: this is not a horse. The medieval unicorn has a goat’s beard, cloven hooves and a lion-like tufted tail — a patchwork animal assembled from ancient descriptions.

Detail · 7 of 8

The fence

A circular paling barely higher than the unicorn’s back. Like the enclosed garden of medieval love poetry, it marks a paradise — a place one stays in willingly.

Detail · 8 of 8

A thousand flowers

The dark millefleur ground is a botanical record: botanists have identified dozens of real plants here, among them wild orchids, Madonna lilies, carnations and wild strawberries — many associated with love and marriage.

V · In fiction

The unicorn goes modern

Once it stopped being a real animal, the unicorn became something better: a character.

  • 1871

    Through the Looking-Glass

    Lewis Carroll

    The Unicorn meets Alice and proposes a bargain: “If you’ll believe in me, I’ll believe in you.”

  • 1922

    Sonnets to Orpheus

    Rainer Maria Rilke

    A sonnet to the creature that does not exist — fed not on grain but on the possibility that it might be.

  • 1956

    The Last Battle

    C. S. Lewis

    Jewel the Unicorn, King Tirian’s dearest friend, rides into the final chapter of Narnia.

  • 1962

    “The Unicorn”

    Shel Silverstein · The Irish Rovers, 1968

    Why are there no unicorns? Because they were too busy playing to board Noah’s ark.

  • 1968

    The Last Unicorn

    Peter S. Beagle

    A unicorn learns she may be the last of her kind and sets out to find the others. Animated in 1982 — a cult classic ever since.

  • 1978

    A Swiftly Tilting Planet

    Madeleine L’Engle

    Charles Wallace rides Gaudior, a winged unicorn, through the winds of time.

  • 1982

    Blade Runner

    Ridley Scott

    A tiny origami unicorn left on the floor — and, in the 1992 Director’s Cut, a unicorn dream — asks whether Deckard is human.

  • 1983

    My Little Pony

    Hasbro

    Unicorn ponies gallop into toy boxes — and, decades later, into one of the most devoted fandoms on the internet.

  • 1985

    Legend

    Ridley Scott

    The Lord of Darkness plots to destroy the last unicorns and plunge the world into eternal night.

  • 1985

    She-Ra: Princess of Power

    Filmation

    Swift Wind, a winged unicorn, carries She-Ra into battle against the Horde.

  • 1997

    Harry Potter and the Philosopher’s Stone

    J. K. Rowling

    In the Forbidden Forest, unicorn blood can keep you alive — at the cost of a cursed half-life.

  • 2010

    Despicable Me

    Illumination

    “It’s so fluffy!” — a carnival prize unicorn becomes a meme for the ages.

  • 2013

    The startup unicorn

    Aileen Lee

    The venture capitalist names billion-dollar startups “unicorns” — rare, magical, and much hunted.

  • 2020

    Onward

    Pixar

    In a world that has forgotten magic, unicorns have become feral, bin-raiding pests. A sad, funny fall from grace.
